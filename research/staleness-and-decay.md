# Staleness and age-based decay as a surfacing or deletion mechanism

Asset for [Prior art: decomposition and honesty in procrastination apps](https://github.com/venkateshrajan/vazhikkal/issues/8).
Produced by a research sub-agent covering vazhikkal's third bet — that a staleness clock can replace due dates entirely.

**Sourcing caveat that applies throughout:** Reddit was fully IP-blocked for this run, and Bing/DuckDuckGo/Mojeek all served CAPTCHAs. There are therefore **no Reddit user reports anywhere in this document**, which is the single biggest hole and it lands exactly where it hurts — streak-loss grief, Habitica damage demoralisation, and Amazing Marvin / OmniFocus staleness UX. Items marked ⚠ are thin or unverified.

## Bottom line

Age-based mechanisms **survive when they decay priority, reset on touch, or prompt review. They get hated or removed when they delete irreversibly and unilaterally.**

Every survivor softens or reverses the penalty: Taskwarrior weights age at one sixth of a due date; `actions/stale` warns before closing; Linear notifies the creator with an unarchive path; Beeminder emails a legitimacy check; Habitica ships an Inn to pause the clock; Todoist's own guidance is archive-over-delete precisely because reversibility is what makes it psychologically bearable. Amazing Marvin proves the badge-only end of the spectrum ships and persists but exerts almost no force.

The sharpest single constraint to adopt is Jacob Tomlinson's rule: **the age clock must only apply pressure to the party whose action is outstanding, and must stop once the item is a validated commitment.** For a single-user app that means the clock attaches to *your next action*, never to the project's mere existence.

## 1. Shipped tools that surface or kill by age

### Trello Card Aging — the strongest evidence that users *want* age decay

Documented feature: "Card aging helps you see cards on a board that haven't been touched in a while." Regular mode makes cards "progressively more transparent and 'fade out' the longer they remain untouched"; Pirate mode makes them "tear, yellow, and crack like an old pirate map" ([Atlassian blog](https://www.atlassian.com/blog/trello/introducing-power-ups-calendar-card-aging-and-more), [support](https://support.atlassian.com/trello/docs/using-the-card-aging-power-up/)). Cards can be marked **Evergreen** to opt out. The card back shows a last-updated timestamp.

⚠ Exact aging thresholds are **not officially documented anywhere findable**; a third-party guide says only "you just measure the age in weeks" ([gtola](https://www.gtola.com/trello-card-aging/)). Do not cite specific day counts.

**The user reaction is the valuable part.** Atlassian announced sunset (new installs blocked 2023-05-30, removal 2023-07-30) and then **reversed it**: "Due to the overwhelming response from the community, the Card Aging Power-up is here to stay!" The objections were specifically that *passive* visibility beat an active filter — "I don't want to have to use a special filter that takes extra steps"; "I sold my team on Trello based on this feature"; "It completely breaks our law firm, which relies on Card Aging to make sure the attorneys move the cards forward" ([Atlassian Community](https://community.atlassian.com/forums/Trello-articles/A-new-way-to-filter-Trello-cards-saying-goodbye-to-the-Card/ba-p/2374997)).

### GitHub stale bots — the backlash in detail

[actions/stale](https://github.com/actions/stale) defaults: **60 days → stale label, +7 days → close.** The clock is `updated_at`, and "any updates made, or any comments added… will restart the counter." At scale, Home Assistant runs `days-before-issue-stale: 90`, `days-before-pr-stale: 60`, both `-close: 7`, exempting `no-stale,help-wanted`, with the candid message "Due to the high number of incoming GitHub notifications, we have to clean some of the old issues…" ([stale.yml](https://github.com/home-assistant/core/blob/dev/.github/workflows/stale.yml)).

The "considered harmful" literature is substantial. Drew DeVault, *GitHub stale bot considered harmful* — "This is a terrible, horrible, no good, very bad idea" — argues maintainers install it out of shame, and "Don't deny your users access to an important mutual support resource" ([drewdevault.com](https://drewdevault.com/blog/stalebot/), [HN 338 pts / 163 comments](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=28998374)). Ben Winding, *GitHub Stale Bots – A False Economy*: closing "Reduce[s] the metric of Open Issues", "Made duplicate issues far more likely", "Ultimately decreased the quality of the software" ([blog](https://blog.benwinding.com/github-stale-bots/), [HN 288 pts / 156 comments](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=25821092)). [nostalebots.xyz](https://nostalebots.xyz/) names 40+ projects and argues activity ≠ priority: "Ignoring issues is better than hiding them, as the stale bot is then essentially a cold face telling the users that you definitely do not care."

The emotional register matters for a self-directed app. Verbatim HN: "All my homies hate stalebot" ([1](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=47524095)); "Fuck stalebot" ([2](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=47523590)). More substantively: "Stale bots disincentivise good quality issues… Bug reports and FRs are also a form of work" ([here](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=47525891)). And on the keep-alive ritual: "The established culture… is that you open an issue, and then you have to keep pinging it every week otherwise the stale bot closes it" ([here](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=48007225)).

**The keep-alive ritual failing is what enrages people.** From Claude Code's own tracker: "A bunch of my bugs reports got closed today for '60 days of inactivity', despite me responding 'this is still relevant'" — **238 👍, 48 comments** ([anthropics/claude-code#16497](https://github.com/anthropics/claude-code/issues/16497)). It spawned a GitHub product-feedback request to let issue authors reopen ([community discussion 182939](https://github.com/orgs/community/discussions/182939)).

**The design-critical nuance, and the best single source in this document.** Jacob Tomlinson: an age clock is legitimate only when the outstanding next action is the *user's* — "A stale bot should never mark something as stale or close it if the responsibility is on the maintainer to take an action," and "Once an issue becomes a clear actionable task it should never be closed due to inactivity" ([jacobtomlinson.dev](https://jacobtomlinson.dev/posts/2024/most-stale-bots-are-anti-user-and-anti-contributor-but-they-dont-have-to-be/)).

**Counter-evidence — deletion as relief for the owner:** "when I was working in enterprise tech support it was a huge relief to close a case and get it off my plate, for any reason" ([HN](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=47527592)). Multiple commenters accept *labelling* but not closing: "There's no problem with stalebot adding a stale label… Adding filters so that developers only look at actionable tickets would be much more sane" ([HN](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=47527893)).

⚠ Could not retrieve the one pro-stale-bot essay ([lonami.dev/blog/stalebots](https://lonami.dev/blog/stalebots/)) — garbled content, archive.org unfetchable.

### Linear / Jira / GitLab

- **Linear (documented).** "When enabled, auto-close will close issues that have not been updated in a set period of time." Crucially it ships a *rescue* affordance: "When an issue archives, its creator will be notified—this is an opportunity to unarchive if the issue is still relevant" ([docs](https://linear.app/docs/configuring-workflows)). No default period published; no changelog announcement; **no user reaction found.**
- **Jira** ⚠ — no named stale-issue feature or automation template found. Mechanism is generic: a [scheduled trigger](https://support.atlassian.com/cloud-automation/docs/jira-automation-triggers/) plus user-written JQL such as `updated < -30d`.
- **GitLab** — no native age-based auto-close ([docs](https://docs.gitlab.com/user/project/issues/managing_issues/)).

### Task apps with real staleness clocks

- **Amazing Marvin — the closest shipped analogue to vazhikkal's design.** *Staleness Warning*: "In the strategy settings you can define at how many days a task and project is considered stale." Note the age runs **from creation, not last touch** — "The moment you create a task or project the day count starts" — and surfaces as "an orange toast icon" ([docs](http://help.amazingmarvin.com/en/articles/1950228-staleness-warning)). *Procrastination Count* is closer to re-commitment: days since first scheduled date, rendered as escalating `!`/`!!`/`!!!`, "orange at first and after 3 days turn red" ([docs](http://help.amazingmarvin.com/en/articles/1950154-procrastination-count)). **No penalty, no demotion, no deletion — badge only.**
- **OmniFocus — touch-to-reset review interval.** "By default, projects are set to be reviewed every week, beginning one week after the project is created," with **Next review / Review every / Last reviewed** fields (Last reviewed "can't be edited"). The Review perspective orders "from oldest at the top" and requires an explicit **Mark Reviewed** to reset ([Inspector](https://support.omnigroup.com/documentation/omnifocus/universal/4.8.11/en/inspector/), [Perspectives](https://support.omnigroup.com/documentation/omnifocus/universal/4.8.11/en/perspectives/)).
- **Todoist.** Real age queries: `created before: -30 days`, `created before: -365 days` ([filters](https://www.todoist.com/help/articles/introduction-to-filters-V98wIH)). Query only, no automation.
- ⚠ **Things 3 / TickTick:** no documented review-interval or age mechanism found. Someday is a manual list only ([Things support](https://culturedcode.com/things/support/)).
- **Taskwarrior — a real decay formula.** `urgency.age.coefficient=2.0` and `urgency.age.max=365` ("After this number of days has elapsed, the urgency of a task won't increase any more because of aging") ([taskrc(5)](https://taskwarrior.org/docs/man/taskrc.5/), [urgency](https://taskwarrior.org/docs/urgency/)). From [Task.cpp](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/GothenburgBitFactory/taskwarrior/stable/src/Task.cpp) the contribution is `min(age_days/365, 1.0) × 2.0` — a linear ramp of roughly 0.0055/day, then flat. Deliberately **weak: 2.0 against `urgency.due.coefficient=12.0`.** No age-based purge exists.
- **GTD — commitment to a *cadence*, not a deadline.** David Allen's own definition: "Someday/Maybe means you are not currently committed to complete it, but you are committed to track it as an item to periodically review for future action" ([gettingthingsdone.com](https://gettingthingsdone.com/2010/10/some-key-gtd-definitions/)); step 4 of the [five steps](https://gettingthingsdone.com/five-steps/) is "Review frequently". ⚠ No primary statement of *why* one week — that argument lives in the books.
- **org-mode habits — a touch *window*, not a deadline.** `STYLE: habit` plus a dual repeater: `.+2d/3d` "says that you want to do the task at least every three days, but, at most, every two days." The consistency graph encodes decay by colour ([manual](https://orgmode.org/manual/Tracking-your-habits.html)). Separately org's [Stuck projects](https://orgmode.org/manual/Stuck-projects.html) is *structural* — no next action — a useful contrast to a temporal clock, and much closer to vazhikkal's bet #2.
- **Kanban Work Item Age — age promoted to a first-class metric.** The [Kanban Guide](https://kanbanguides.org/english/) makes it **mandatory**, one of four Flow Metrics: "The elapsed time between when a work item started and the current date." Vendor operationalisation: average age of WIP, Aging Charts ([Nave](https://getnave.com/blog/average-age-of-work-in-progress/)). ⚠ ActionableAgile/Vacanti primary pages 404 — cite kanbanguides.org.
- **Anki — resurfacing by predicted decay rather than by date.** "Desired retention controls how likely you are to remember cards… The default value of 90% will schedule cards so you have a 90% chance of remembering them when they come up for review again" ([deck options](https://docs.ankiweb.net/deck-options.html), [studying](https://docs.ankiweb.net/studying.html)).

### Auto-deleting / ephemeral todo apps — the most valuable negative result

**Every one found is dead.** *Tet*, "a todo app that deletes your tasks at the end of the day" ([HN, 86 pts / 45 comments](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=16316344)) — domain 404. *Gone: The ephemeral to-do list* ([HN](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=15218163)) — 503. *Kill List*, "tasks deletes if not done by midnight" ([HN](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=46387499)) — 404. [Ephe](https://github.com/unvalley/ephe) (143 stars) is alive but ephemerality is framing only, with no documented auto-deletion. **No currently-shipping, maintained todo product whose core mechanic is auto-expiring tasks was found.** Structured / Amie / Sunsama have no decay mechanic.

**The split reaction, verbatim from the Tet thread.** For: "It's impossible for a task backlog to start forming. Every day is a clean slate"; "creates pressure to actually do the things on the list… Natural pruning saves wasted time later"; "add items to TODO lists is sometimes just a form of procrastinating." Against: "This would just give me huge anxiety. One reason I write things down in a todo list is to remove the anxiety of having to remember everything"; "it would make you stress more about getting your things done before it deletes them"; "Not all tasks are designed to be done in one day."

**Two real-world decay systems that *survived*, both softer than deletion:** "At a previous job we practiced aggressive, automated ticket de-prioritization. Every N days of inactivity, a ticket would drop one priority point… The theory was that anything that was truly high priority would have been done"; and the Ballmer anecdote of tearing the todo list in half every six months, on the logic that anything important gets re-added.

### Streak and decay pressure, and how users react

- **Duolingo (published primary data).** "learners who reach a streak of 7 are 2.4 times more likely to continue using Duolingo the next day than learners without a streak." Decoupling streak from daily goal produced "3.3% increase in Day 14 retention", "1% increase in our overall daily active learners", "10.5% increase in the percentage of daily learners on a streak" (19% for new learners); a year on, "just over half of our daily learners have a streak at least 7 days long, compared to about a third… a year ago" ([Improving the streak](https://blog.duolingo.com/improving-the-streak/)). **Protective mechanics measurably work:** Streak Wager gave "Day-7 retention… +14%"; Weekend Amulet users were "4% more likely to come back a week later" and "5% less likely to lose their streak", against weekend DAU declines "by as much as 5-10%" ([How Streaks keep learners committed](https://blog.duolingo.com/how-streaks-keep-duolingo-learners-committed-to-their-language-goals/)). ⚠ Duolingo publishes nothing on the emotional cost of streak *loss*.
- **Habitica (user report).** Damage pile-up from missed Dailies is documented as demoralising in the project's own tracker: "we've been getting extremely frustrated because the rewards have never outweighed the losses from dying 5 times per quest. We have a level 60 healer who can't keep up with damage done from missed dailies" — and damage hits the whole party ([HabitRPG/habitica#3161](https://github.com/HabitRPG/habitica/issues/3161)). The pause valve, *Rest in the Inn*, exists: "A player may rest in the Inn when they are ill, on vacation, or unable to complete their Dailies" — ⚠ search-snippet only, habitica.fandom.com returns HTTP 402 here.
- **Beeminder (documented).** The commitment path is "the steepness of the Bright Red Line (previously 'Yellow Brick Road')". On derailment: "You get an email asking if the derailment was legit. You're automatically charged the amount of your pledge. Your graph is automatically rerailed at a higher pledge amount, up to your pledge cap." Escalation ladder **$5, $10, $30, $90, $270, $810, $2430**, designed so "you'll never waste more than half of the amount that eventually motivates you to stay on track". Akrasia horizon is about a week: "to beat akrasia you only need to bind yourself for whatever the horizon on 'immediate' is" ([faq](https://www.beeminder.com/faq)). **The legit-check email is the single most transferable design element for a penalty clock.**

## 2. How users react when a system deletes or archives by age

- **Google inactive accounts (documented, closest structural match to a staleness clock).** "An inactive Google Account is an account that has not been used within a 2-year period. Google reserves the right to delete an inactive Google Account and its activity and data." Note the explicit enumerated list of **what counts as a touch** — reading or sending mail, using Drive, watching a video, a search — plus advance email warnings and carve-outs for accounts with purchases ([support](https://support.google.com/accounts/answer/12418290)). The enumerated-touch-list is directly applicable: a staleness clock has to define its reset event unambiguously.
- **Slack 90-day free-tier hiding** ([pricing update](https://slack.com/intl/en-gb/blog/news/pricing-and-plan-updates), [HN 26 pts / 22 comments](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=32251177)). ⚠ Thin — the thread is small and the top comment is actually *sanguine*: "by the time they reach the limit, the information is stale that perhaps no one will miss it" ([here](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=32270638)). The large-scale outrage this brief anticipated was not found.
- **Decay as relief, from a task-app vendor itself — the best single source for the trust argument.** Todoist's [*I Declare To-Do List Bankruptcy!*](https://www.todoist.com/inspiration/todo-list-bankruptcy). On backlog guilt: a refreshed list "reflect[s] my priorities… rather than guilt-inducing – 'here are all the things I said I wanted to do and didn't'." On debt: "there will be a lot of things that make it onto your to-do list that you're never going to do… That is your to-do list debt." On the failure mode: "Rick's projects became so overgrown with irrelevant tasks, he stopped opening them" — and "I was never going to come back to them anyway." **Load-bearing finding: "archiving all your tasks and projects is mentally much easier than deleting… You could, theoretically, get them back if you missed them, but you won't."** No quantitative data — single anecdote.
- **Email bankruptcy.** The practice is "deleting or ignoring all emails older than a certain date, due to an overwhelming volume of messages"; "usually attributed to author Lawrence Lessig in 2004", also "attributed to Sherry Turkle in 2002" ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Email_bankruptcy)). ⚠ The primary 43folders post is unreachable (expired TLS cert), so attribution is second-hand.

## 3. Due dates as an anti-pattern

**Ariely & Wertenbroch 2002 is the strongest single result for vazhikkal's design** (*Psychological Science* 13(3):219–224; [full PDF](https://web.mit.edu/ariely/www/MIT/Papers/deadlines.pdf)). Their own three answers: people *do* self-impose costly deadlines (yes), those deadlines *do* improve performance (yes), but "Do people set their deadlines optimally? … the answer to the last question is 'no'."

- **Study 1** — 99 MIT exec-ed professionals; free-choice binding deadlines with a 1%/day grade penalty versus fixed evenly-spaced. Only 32% of deadlines were set in the final week and only 27% dumped all three papers on the last day, so people genuinely do pre-commit. But grades were **higher under externally imposed evenly-spaced deadlines (M = 88.76) than self-chosen (M = 85.67), t(97) = 3.03, p = .003**; spillover to the final project was larger still (M = 86 vs 77, t(95) = 4.15, p < .001).
- **Diagnosis** — restricting analysis to students who happened to space their own deadlines evenly made the gap non-significant, **reducing effect size by 59%.** The deficit is *bad spacing*, not a weaker force from self-imposition.
- **Study 2** — 60 proofreaders, 3 texts, 10¢/error, $1/day late. Errors detected ranked evenly-spaced > self-imposed > single end deadline, F(2,38) = 63.28, p < .001; delays roughly 3.4 / 8 / 13 days; earnings roughly $20 / $13 / $4. Restricting to the n=10 who self-spaced evenly cut effect sizes by 55–79%.
- **The caveat to design around:** the best-performing regime was the *least liked*. Enjoyment ran inverse to performance — evenly-spaced M = 22.1, self-imposed M = 28.12, end-deadline M = 37.9, F(2,57) = 17.06, p < .001.
- **Implication for vazhikkal:** an every-N-days staleness clock *is* an externally imposed, evenly-spaced deadline schedule — precisely the condition that beat self-set due dates. **Expect it to work and to be resented.**

**David Allen on the calendar** is exactly the anti-fake-due-date position: only "time-specific actions", "day-specific actions" and "day-specific information" belong there, because "the calendar should be sacred territory. If you write something there, it must get done that day or not at all" — the "hard landscape" of fixed commitments with real consequences. ⚠ Only obtainable via a secondary source quoting *Getting Things Done* (2001) — [scheduleu.org](https://scheduleu.org/why-david-allen-seems-to-be-changing-his-mind-about-scheduling/) — which also argues Allen softened by 2008–2016 ("if you've ever committed to a meeting on your calendar, you've already acknowledged the value of a time block…Block away!"). Verify against the book before quoting.

**Defer versus due, formally** (OmniFocus glossary): Defer Date = "The date and time that an item becomes Available for work… a 'Start No Earlier Than' constraint"; Due Date = "…must be completed… an 'End No Later Than' constraint" ([glossary](https://support.omnigroup.com/documentation/omnifocus/universal/4.8.11/en/glossary/)). Useful vocabulary for the domain model: **a staleness clock is neither — it is a recurring start constraint with a penalty.**

**Alert habituation — analogue evidence, not task-app evidence.** The canonical quantification: over 31 days across five UCSF adult ICUs, "2,558,760 unique audible and inaudible… alarms", "an average alarm burden of 187 audible alarms per bed per day", and **"88.8% of the 12,671 annotated arrhythmia alarms were false positives"** ([Drew et al. 2014, PLOS ONE](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0110274)). Clinical decision support shows the dismissal endpoint: alert "efficiency" — the share leading to any intervention — measured at **4.0%** for anticoagulant alerts ([Br J Clin Pharmacol 2025](https://doi.org/10.1111/bcp.16236)). Alarm fatigue is formally "clinicians being desensitized to the high volume of monitoring alarms" ([Nurs Crit Care 2025](https://doi.org/10.1111/nicc.13302)). ⚠ This is a strong *mechanistic analogue* for "fake due dates train you to ignore alerts", but no study was found testing habituation to self-imposed task-app due dates directly.

## 4. "N days since last touched" as a shipped metric

Age-since-last-touch is thoroughly normalised outside todo apps, which de-risks the concept: Trello Card Aging plus board activity filters (last week / two weeks / four weeks / no activity in four weeks); GitHub's `updated_at` driving `actions/stale`; OmniFocus's non-editable **Last reviewed**; Todoist's `created before: -30 days`; Taskwarrior's `age` column and age→urgency ramp; the Kanban Guide's mandatory **Work Item Age**; and AWS IAM **last accessed information**, which reports "the date and time when the attempt to access an AWS API was made" over a ≥400-day window explicitly so admins "identify unused permissions so that you can remove them" ([AWS docs](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/IAM/latest/UserGuide/access_policies_access-advisor.html)). ⚠ Notion documents only *sorting* by last-edited, not relative last-edited filters ([help](https://www.notion.com/help/views-filters-and-sorts)).
